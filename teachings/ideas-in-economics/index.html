Economics is a way of understanding the world — and the fastest way to learn it is to do it. Each unit builds from the core optimisation framework (how people and firms make the best choice they can), applies it to real data, and offers an optional film and Nobel case for context. The course is split evenly into a microeconomics half (choice, firms, markets, market failure) and a macroeconomics half (measuring the economy, growth, money and crises), so it builds the conceptual and empirical foundation you carry into Microeconomics-I (ECON 201) and Macroeconomics-I (ECON 202).

Teaching Philosophy

I teach introductory economics as a tool you learn by using. Each unit starts from the economist’s core question — given scarce resources, what is the best choice, and what happens when everyone makes it? — develops the formal framework to answer it (the optimisation and equilibrium spine of Acemoglu, Laibson & List), and then puts that framework to work on real data. Every unit ends with a short “Doing Economics” exercise on an actual dataset — Indian wherever possible. My emphasis is economic reasoning and evidence over memorisation: the textbook supplies the rigour, the data work gives you a skill — reading, plotting, and interpreting real numbers — and an optional film and Nobel-laureate case supplies the intuition and the human story. That combination is what you carry straight into Microeconomics-I and Macroeconomics-I.

Objectives

  • Introduce core micro- and macroeconomic concepts through the optimisation-and-equilibrium framework, connecting abstract ideas to concrete situations.
  • Develop economic reasoning and basic empirical skill — reading, plotting, and interpreting real data to answer an economic question.
  • Connect each unit to landmark Nobel Prize-winning research and an optional film case, linking popular stories to frontier scholarship.
  • Apply both halves to the Indian context — prices, labour, informality, poverty, growth, and the environment — using Indian datasets.
  • Build the conceptual and empirical foundation for further study in Microeconomics-I and Macroeconomics-I.

Course Outcomes

By the end of the course, students should be able to:

  • Identify and explain foundational concepts — scarcity, opportunity cost, supply and demand, market equilibrium, and externalities.
  • Analyse economic behaviour and institutional dynamics using formal reasoning, and connect that reasoning to narrative cases drawn from film and current events.
  • Evaluate the contributions of Nobel Prize-winning economists and relate their research to real-world phenomena and policy debates.
  • Apply economic frameworks to contemporary issues in the Indian economy — informality, labour markets, poverty, and environmental challenges.

Course Structure — a Micro half & a Macro half

Six units, three contact hours each. The first three build the microeconomics foundation (→ ECON 201); the second three build the macroeconomics foundation (→ ECON 202). Every unit = a theory core (Acemoglu, Laibson & List, supported by CORE), a short “Doing Economics” exercise on real data, and an optional film + Nobel case for context.

◀ MICRO HALF — how people & firms decide (prepares for Microeconomics-I)

Unit What you'll learn · math core · data · readings
Unit 1 Choice, Preferences & Strategic Behaviour3 hours
  • Scarcity, opportunity cost, preferences, utility; rational choice & strategic interaction — the idea of equilibrium
  • 🧮 Math core: utility maximisation, indifference curves, best-response & Nash equilibrium
  • 📊 Data: run a class game (prisoner's dilemma / public goods); compute cooperation vs. the Nash prediction
📖 ReadALL Ch 3, 5, 13 · CORE (choice & strategic interaction) · Begg (consumer choice)  ·  🎬 OptionalA Beautiful Mind · Nash
Unit 2 Firms, Markets & Competition3 hours

  • 🧮 Math core: cost curves, profit maximisation (MR = MC), consumer & producer surplus
  • 📊 Data: pull daily mandi prices (Agmarknet) through a supply shock and tell the demand–supply story
📖 ReadALL Ch 6, 7, 12 · CORE (firm & price-taking) · Begg (costs & market structure)  ·  🎬 OptionalMargin Call · Krugman
Unit 3 Externalities, Welfare & Market Failure3 hours
  • Externalities, public goods, the commons; welfare loss and the case for policy
  • 🧮 Math core: deadweight loss, Pigouvian tax, private vs. social cost
  • 📊 Data: plot your city's air quality (CPCB) against a pollution source; "price" the externality
📖 ReadALL Ch 9, 10 · CORE (property, power & the commons) · Begg (market failure & welfare)  ·  🎬 OptionalErin Brockovich · Ostrom

▶ MACRO HALF — how the whole economy behaves (prepares for Macroeconomics-I)

Unit What you'll learn · math core · data · readings
Unit 4 Measuring the Economy & Development3 hours

  • 🧮 Math core: the GDP identity, real vs. nominal, price-index numbers
  • 📊 Data: measure poverty / income from PLFS or World Bank data — and ask what GDP leaves out

Unit 5 Growth & Productivity3 hours
  • Production and productivity at the economy level; data-driven decisions; what drives long-run growth
  • 🧮 Math core: the aggregate production function, capital accumulation, the Solow steady state
  • 📊 Data: plot India vs. South Korea GDP per capita since 1950 (Maddison); discuss catch-up

Total contact hours: 18. Each unit also carries a dedicated Indian Context essay. The six exercises are rehearsals for the End-Term Empirical Project.

Where it Leads — Microeconomics-I & Macroeconomics-I

This course is designed to flow into the Year-2 core. The micro half front-loads the ideas ECON 201 builds on; the macro half does the same for ECON 202. You meet the optimisation-and-equilibrium framework and its graphs here; the full calculus behind it is carried by ECON 122 (Mathematics for Economics) — this course gives you the concepts, the framework, and the data skill, with the heavy mathematics deferred to ECON 122.

Readings, Resources & Data

The course is built on one modern principles textbook, supported by two free, open-access companions for the concepts and the data work, with a traditional text kept on the shelf as a theory reference. Specific chapters are assigned per unit and posted on MOODLE.

The spine — three books

  • PrimaryAcemoglu, Laibson & List — Economics (Pearson). The backbone of every unit: a modern, evidence-based text organised around three principles — optimisation, equilibrium, empiricism — that carries the formal and mathematical content.
  • SupportCORE Econ — The Economy 2.0. Free, open-access; the applied, data-first companion — development- and India-aware — that grounds each unit in real-world questions.
  • DataCORE Econ — Doing Economics. Free empirical-projects book; each project is a ready-made real-data exercise (the model this course is named after).
  • ReferenceBegg, Vernasca, Fischer & Dornbusch — Economics (McGraw-Hill). Kept as an exam-style theory reference — clean graphs and derivations for consumer choice, costs, market structures, and IS–LM — for students heading toward an LSE-style second year.

Course Policies

Suggested Pedagogy

Each unit opens with the core framework — a short lecture and worked examples from Acemoglu, Laibson & List, supported by CORE — then unpacks it through guided discussion, and closes with a short “Doing Economics” exercise on real data. Browser-based simulations let students experiment with supply and demand, externalities, and game theory in real time; Indian Context essays and datasets ground the material locally; and an optional feature film and a Nobel-laureate case connect the framework to a popular story and to frontier scholarship.

Attendance & Participation

A minimum of 75% attendance is required to be eligible for the End-Term assessment, per FLAME University policy. Participation is graded on engagement in discussions, film screenings, and in-class activities — the goal is to think out loud, not to be “tested.” Be kind and respectful when addressing others.

Assessments & Late Submission

  • Problem Sets (20%) — four to five sets distributed across the semester; applied economic reasoning on the framework, worked scenarios, and real data.
  • Mid-Term Examination (30%) — covers the micro half (Units 1–3); conceptual questions, short-answer problems, and data interpretation.
  • End-Term Empirical Project (40%) — a group “Doing Economics” study applying course concepts to a real-world question on real data, with a written report.

Deadlines are firm. Late submissions lose 10% of the component’s marks per day and are not accepted more than three days past the deadline, except with a documented, valid reason discussed in advance.

Academic Integrity

All work you submit must be your own. Plagiarism, unauthorised collusion, fabrication of results, and contract cheating are serious violations handled under FLAME University’s academic-integrity regulations. Whenever you draw on a source — a textbook, article, dataset, film, or website — cite it clearly and consistently. If you are unsure what needs a citation, ask before submitting.

Use of Generative AI

Generative-AI tools may not be used to produce any part of your graded submissions — problem sets, the empirical project report, or exam answers — unless I explicitly permit it for a given task; doing otherwise is treated as an academic-integrity violation. You are, however, encouraged to use AI for your own learning — to explain difficult concepts, test your understanding, or explore ideas outside assessed work. AI tools make confident mistakes, so verifying anything they tell you is your own responsibility: treat their output as a starting point to check against the readings and lectures, never as an authoritative source.

Student Wellbeing & Accessibility

Your well-being matters more than any single grade. Students with a disability or any condition affecting participation or assessment are entitled to reasonable accommodations — contact the relevant FLAME student-support office and me, in confidence, as early as possible. If personal, health, or financial difficulties are affecting your studies, please reach out; seeking help early will never count against you.
