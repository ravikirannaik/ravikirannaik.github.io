<p class="nob-lede">
The 2026 Economics Nobel went to Robert Hall and Michael Woodford for building macroeconomics up from the choices of individual households and firms. Here is what they won for, and where it lands in our syllabus, from the consumption function and unemployment to money, forward guidance, and IS&ndash;LM. Optional background, not for assessment.
</p>

## 1. What just happened

On 12 October 2026 the Economics Nobel went to **Robert Hall** (Stanford) and **Michael Woodford** (Columbia), "for microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilisation." That phrase is dry. But it describes something you have been doing all semester without being told it had a name.

Look at how our course is built. The first half is about individuals and single markets: a production function, a worker choosing how much to work, a household choosing how much to consume and save, a firm choosing how much to invest. That is microeconomics, sitting inside a macro course. Module 5 then pulls back to the whole economy at once, with IS–LM, aggregate demand and supply, money and inflation. That is macroeconomics.

For most of the twentieth century these two halves did not fit together. Keynes gave us a working macro engine in the 1930s, but it ran on relationships between big aggregates (consumption rises with income, and so on) that were not derived from any actual person deciding anything. Physicists have their dream of a "theory of everything" that joins the physics of the very small to the physics of the very large. Economics had the same split, and closing it turned out to be hard.

It cracked open in the 1970s, when the old models could not explain high inflation and high unemployment happening together (stagflation). Robert Lucas then landed the punch now known as the **Lucas critique**: a historical correlation that is not built on how people actually decide will fall apart the moment a policymaker leans on it. (Close cousin of Goodhart's law: once a measure becomes a target, it stops behaving.) After that, macro had to be rebuilt from the individual up, and Hall and Woodford did more than almost anyone to rebuild it.

## 2. What each of them showed

**Hall, and the random walk of consumption (1978).** You met the consumption function in Module 3. Hall took Friedman's permanent-income idea and made it precise: if households look ahead and already use all the information they have, their consumption should follow a *random walk*, and only genuine surprises should move it. Here is the classroom version. A one-off Diwali bonus barely changes your monthly spending; a permanent raise changes it a lot. Most of us behave roughly like that, and Hall was the one who pinned down why.

**Hall, and why downturns show up as unemployment.** In a 2005 paper he asked why jobs disappear in a slump. His answer runs straight back to Module 2: a job is a *match* between one worker and one employer, and wages do not fall quickly or smoothly. So when demand drops, firms do not cut wages, they stop making new matches. That is unemployment, and it has its own moving parts. It is the frictional and structural unemployment from your lectures, not a number the central bank can simply dial.

**Woodford, and managing expectations (2003).** His book *Interest and Prices* became the manual for modern central banking, and it points straight at Modules 4 and 5. Woodford showed that a central bank steers the economy less by what it does today than by what people expect it to do tomorrow. Expectations are the lever. That is the theory behind **forward guidance**, where a central bank talks about its future plans to change behaviour now.

Words really can be policy. In 2012 the ECB's Mario Draghi said the bank would do "whatever it takes" to save the euro. He spent nothing that day; markets calmed on the promise alone. During COVID in 2020, the US Fed and the RBI both signalled that rates would stay low for as long as needed, and spending and investment moved on the signal. That is Woodford's lever in a sentence.

## 3. Why it is a big deal (and one live twist)

Their work is why the models now sitting inside central banks, the RBI included, are built from households and firms rather than from loose aggregates. When 2008 and the 2020 pandemic hit, this was the toolkit governments reached for.

Here is the twist worth watching. Woodford is the theoretical father of forward guidance. Yet the prize lands in the very year the new US Fed chair, Kevin Warsh, has moved to scrap it, saying it has "overstayed its welcome." A Nobel hands out credit; it does not end the argument. Worth sitting with: a prize-winning idea can still be up for debate.

## 4. The course map: where this prize lives

| Prize idea | Where you meet it in ECON 202 |
| :-- | :-- |
| Consumption as a random walk (Hall 1978) | Module 3, consumption, saving, determinants |
| Jobs as matches, sticky wages, unemployment (Hall 2005) | Module 2, labour-market equilibrium, unemployment types, Okun's Law |
| Expectations and the interest-rate lever (Woodford) | Module 4, money, QTM, money growth and inflation |
| Forward guidance, monetary policy, money neutrality | Module 5, IS–LM and AD–AS |
| Why we build macro from micro at all (Lucas critique) | Week 17, schools of thought, business cycles |

So you have *already* been doing Hall, back in Modules 2 and 3. Woodford is coming: when we reach **IS–LM** in Module 5, the LM curve and the monetary-policy story are exactly where forward guidance lives, and the money-neutrality debate there is the modern face of the classical-versus-Keynesian argument our textbook (Abel) is organised around.

## 5. An India footnote

These models assume an economy of banks, formal credit and salaried jobs, where a rate cut moves lending and a sticky wage governs hiring. India has that world only in part. Most Indian workers are informal or self-employed, which is one reason a repo-rate change reaches people slowly here. The models describe India's organised sector well, and that sector is growing, but keep the gap in mind when we get to policy. For a developing economy, the prize reads as much like a to-do list as a trophy.

When we draw our first IS–LM diagram in Module 5, I will ask you to remember this: behind those two clean lines sit the households and firms Hall and Woodford insisted we start from. Keep that picture. It is most of what the 2026 prize is about.
